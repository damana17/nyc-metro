2. Why: the first buyer gets the cheap shares

Every share sold raises that line’s price by $15, and a share pays the same dividend whatever you paid for it. Seat 1 opens by buying three lines at $10 each. Later seats pay $25 to $55 for theirs, and the gap compounds through 20-odd dividend calls. Take the step away and the edge flips to the later seats, so the step is the lever. Smaller steps do not help: the advantage holds until the step is zero.

4. The Tally rewards buying shares in a dead line

The app’s Tally values each share at the current price, and the price rises with every purchase. Buying a share therefore raises the value of every share you already hold. One player buying eight shares of a line that never pays a dividend gains — on the Tally. Valued at the base price, the same purchases cost —.

Cash-only scoring keeps the lead moving until about round 10, because buying a share costs score until it pays back. But it is still decided by seat, so the tension is partly an accounting effect. Base-price scoring gives the most building, with the winner usually ahead by round 3.

A player who never lays track or builds stations almost never wins, so the neutral lines do not invite free-riding. Never calling dividends costs nothing under most rules, because rivals call them anyway and the payout reaches everyone. That makes the dividend call a weak decision, except under cash-only scoring.

Settings that barely matter: draw size (the 10-disc hand limit binds first), the crossing rule (lines rarely meet) and action points from 4 to 6. Station base mostly sets how many stations get built. At the app’s default of 3, half the board starts with free stations. At 5, the “5 minus distance” of the design notes, about half as many are built.

Recommendations

  1. Change the final score before the next playtest. Value shares at the base price, which is the price without the per-sale step, or use majority/minority bonuses on that base price. Do not use the Tally’s ladder value: it pays players for buying anything.
  2. Fix the opening. Limit shares per turn to one or two and move the start player each round (snake order or rotation). In simulation that took the first seat from about 80% of wins to about a third at 3 players. Play-test it, and test 4 players separately.
  3. Give the dividend call a cost or an owner. Today it is a free action that helps everyone. Options: the caller gets a bonus, only the caller’s lines pay, or calling costs the whole turn.
  4. Decide the station base. The app uses 3, the design notes say 5. Five builds about half the stations and leaves more discs in the bag.
  5. Drop or rework settings that do nothing. Draw size and crossing cost change little while the hand limit is 10 and lines rarely cross.
How the simulation works, and its limits

The engine re-implements the app’s rules mode and imports the app’s map, zones and geometry. It was checked against the app’s own functions on 869,659 positions with no differences. Where the app leaves a rule to the table, the engine assumes: action points spent automatically; investing (up to 3 shares) and calling dividends share one slot per turn; the game ends after 15 rounds. Merges are not simulated because the app does not implement them.

The bots are greedy: each action point they take the move that most improves a projection of their final standing against the table. Five weights in that projection were tuned by self-play separately for each scoring rule. Tuned bots beat random play every time. They do not plan several turns ahead, bluff, or negotiate, and they all think alike, which can exaggerate an edge that a varied human table would blunt. Treat the sizes of effects as indicative and the directions as solid.
